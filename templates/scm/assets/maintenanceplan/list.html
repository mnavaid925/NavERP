Supply Chain›Maintenance Plans

A plan is a definition, not an event: one row per recurring job a machine owes — the greasing round, the quarterly inspection, the 500-hour service, the vibration threshold that says look at it now. Nothing here executes. Pressing Generate snapshots a work order, and that job is what gets scheduled, worked and costed, which is what makes “the plan changed last month” and “this is what the job actually said in March” both answerable.

The due filter is a date query and says so: a plan is due because of its next due date and its own call horizon, both of which are columns. The usage axes cannot be expressed in SQL at all — a meter or condition plan is due because of the asset's latest reading, which lives in another table at another grain. So a plan that is overdue only on its meter still renders its own red chip in the unfiltered list and is simply not reachable through ?due=overdue. The chip per row tells the whole truth; the filter narrows on the date.

No maintenance plans

Nothing is scheduled against a machine yet, so nothing will ever come due on its own. Author a plan on an asset — every N days, every N meter units, both (whichever arrives first), or when a reading crosses a threshold — give it a checklist, and its jobs can be raised from one button with the steps already on them. If you were expecting rows here, try clearing the filters.

Nothing on this screen writes: there is no stored “is overdue” flag for a page load to catch up, because every chip above is recomputed from the schedule and the machine's latest reading on every read. Generate is the only action here that creates anything — one explicit human press, inside one transaction, never a background auto-post; an ERP that quietly raises work orders overnight produces a queue nobody trusts. A plan with an open job refuses a second generate, because a duplicate would split one service's checklist, parts and cost across two documents with nothing afterwards able to tell they were the same occurrence.
