A plan is a definition — everything on this form is an input, and nothing here executes. The trigger decides when the work comes due, the schedule fields decide where the cadence currently stands, and the last group is what a generated job gets stamped with. How it comes due

Each trigger reads only its own inputs, and the save is refused if they are missing — a plan that cannot come due is worse than no plan, because it sits on the register looking like the machine is covered and never raises a single job. The three axis blocks below say which trigger uses them; a block greyed out is not used by the trigger currently selected and is left alone rather than cleared, so switching back does not lose what you typed.

Floating measures the next occurrence from the last completion, so a service done a fortnight late pushes the whole future schedule a fortnight out — right for wear-driven work. Fixed measures from the calendar the plan already published, so a late job does not drag every future cycle late with it — right for a statutory inspection due in January whatever happened in December. The call horizon is how far ahead of the due date a job may be raised so the storeroom and the technician have notice; it is what the “Due soon” chip means, and it never makes work owed.

The cadence is in the asset's own meter units, and the asset must have a meter named on its record — a meter plan on a machine with no meter is an empty claim, because readings would have nothing to attach to and the gap would answer blank forever. Both halves or neither: a threshold with no operator cannot be evaluated, and an operator with no threshold has nothing to compare against. There is deliberately no “equals” — a continuous reading almost never lands exactly on a threshold, so an equality trigger would look configured and never fire. A condition plan with no reading at all stays quiet rather than raising a job off silence: an unmeasured machine is unknown, not healthy and not failing.

Where the schedule stands now

These two are the schedule itself, not a derivation — which is why they are editable at all. Set the first occurrence here; afterwards the generate action and the work-order complete verb roll them forward by exactly one cycle each, even when several were missed, so a plan three cycles behind stays visibly behind rather than being silently caught up. A planner may still shift either by hand, and that is the point of them being fields. A calendar plan needs only the date; a meter plan only the reading; a combined plan both.

What a generated job inherits

Every generated work order is stamped with these. Planned labour is carried onto the job as a stated line in its description and is never written into the job's labour hours — labour hours are the actual figure the job's cost, the asset's maintenance-cost-to-date and the depreciation report's repair-vs-replace ratio are all computed from, and pre-filling it would put a real cost on a job nobody has worked yet. The storeroom is only named here: parts leave stock at the job's own issue action, never from this form.

Three blank rows are always offered, so a step can be added by hand or re-added after deletion and a brand-new plan can gain its first one. Leave a row blank to skip it; tick Remove to delete a saved step. Number the steps in tens — 10, 20, 30 — so one can be inserted between two later without renumbering the sheet. A mandatory step is one the job cannot be signed off without; optional steps exist so a technician is never forced to tick something that did not apply, which is what keeps the ticks on the mandatory ones worth reading. A safety step — isolation, lock-out/tag-out, permit — is flagged rather than filed in a separate permit register, so it stays visible and printable on the job itself. These steps are copied onto a generated job with no link back, so editing them next month cannot rewrite what a job three weeks ago actually asked somebody to check and sign against. Deleting a plan cascades them away and they cannot be reconstructed.
