Leave the site blank to plan the whole network. A plan scoped to a warehouse or a zone reads volume from that location and everything beneath it, because the ledger is written at bin level while a plan is written at site level — filtering on the site exactly would produce a grid of zeroes for a perfectly ordinary plan and read as "this site has no work". The grain is the staffing decision being made: daily is what a shift manager actually decides, weekly is the seasonal look-ahead. There is deliberately no hourly grain — an hourly grid over a quarter is a row count nobody reviews.

The period is capped at 366 buckets — a year of days, or seven years of weeks — because the grid is one database row per bucket per in-scope standard, and an unbounded span is an unbounded bulk insert any planner could fire by pressing Generate. The span is measured by arithmetic on the two dates rather than by building the range, so a period that is too long is refused without ever allocating the list the bound exists to prevent. A weekly plan keeps your start weekday rather than snapping to Monday: moving it would mis-align every bucket against the volume history read for it.

Where the volume comes from

The volume source names a ledger this system already keeps, and the history is derived when Generate is pressed and never stored — so a regenerate always reflects what actually happened rather than a stale copy of it. Each activity is driven by the flow that produces its work: receiving and put-away read what came in, picking, packing, loading, replenishment and value-added service read what went out, and cycle counting reads both added together, because counting workload tracks how much the site moves rather than which direction it moved in.

The forecast and the volume source are paired both ways and a mismatch is refused as a field error: a forecast-driven plan with no forecast attached would generate an empty grid and look like a site with no work, while a forecast hung off a stock-moves plan is a pointer nothing consults — which is worse than none, because the header would name a forecast the numbers on the page were never derived from. Same period last year looks back 364 days rather than 365, so the comparison bucket lands on the same weekday: a Monday is not a Saturday's workload.

How volume becomes people

These two are the divisors behind every required headcount the generator will write: required minutes = standard minutes × 100 ÷ productivity, then headcount = required minutes ÷ (hours per shift × 60). At 90% productivity a bucket costing 1,000 standard minutes needs 1,111 real ones, which is the honest number to roster against — planning to the standard and hoping is how a site is understaffed every day and nobody can point at the assumption that did it. Neither may be zero, and that is not an arbitrary floor: a zero here is a division by zero inside the generator for every line in the grid, so the minimum is enforced on the field rather than discovered at the press of a button.
